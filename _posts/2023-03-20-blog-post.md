Water, one of the elements in the Five Agents, carries a variety of cultural connotations in traditional Chinese as well as East Asian contexts. As a result, it is not uncommon to notice it being utilized in literary and philosophical texts, both explicitly, to illustrate abstract ideas, and inadvertently, as an embodiment of the author’s ideology. Chuang Tzu, a Taoist philosopher who lived during the Warring States Period in ancient China, has extensively and repeatedly incorporated water as a symbol of abstract concepts in his fables. In this paper, I will critically examine the major instances of water as symbolism in Chuang Tzu’s philosophical ideas, specifically the Way, inaction and non-dependence, and how these concepts interact to support the central theme of freedom. Through the analysis, one could shed light on the intricate relationship between Taoist philosophy and the natural world and thus gain a deeper understanding of how Chuang Tzu’s concept of freedom and Taoist enlightenment is rooted in his observations of the breadth, fluidity and adaptability of water.

As Taoism emphasizes living by the laws of nature and spontaneity, Chuang Tzu frequently places his ideas in the context of nature. He incorporates the characteristics of water to represent various facets of the Way, an ontologically nebulous concept that alludes to the natural force or order in the cosmos. It is also the ultimate goal or realm in Taoist philosophy. Chuang Tzu begins “Autumn Floods” with a stirring scene:

> The time of the autumn floods came and the hundred streams poured into the Yellow River. Its racing current swelled to such proportions that, looking from bank to bank or island to island, it was impossible to distinguish a horse from a cow ... he could see no end to the water (Chuang Tzu, 96).

It resonates with the opening of “Free and Easy Wandering,” where the huge K’un in the northern darkness transforms into P’eng and sets off to the Lake of Heaven (23). The Chinese word for “darkness,” *ming*, could also be translated as “dark sea,” an extensive body of water. Here, we can understand the floods, the North Sea, *ming*, the Lake of Heaven and all bodies of water as physical embodiments of the Way. The sea, specifically, “never changes” and “is never empty” (97), which is commonly described as a constant in the universe. The vastness, unboundedness, expansiveness, consistency, infiniteness, and power of the Way are manifested through these examples, since the idea closely resembles how water behaves in the natural world.

Not only does Chuang Tzu use water in nature’s setting to explain the Way itself, but also to demonstrate the concept of inaction. In the fable about stranded fish in “The Great and Venerable Teacher,” he suggests that it is better for the fish to "forget each other in the rivers and lakes” instead of “wet(ting) each other with spit” (76). The instance of water is a symbol of the Way. He is supporting the point that it is better to “transform yourself with the way” instead of “praising Yao and condemning Chieh” (76). In this example, both praising Yao and condemning Chieh are “actions.” They are products of the external world. Later in the text, he explicitly states that “[t]he Way has its reality and its signs but is without action or form” (77). Chuang Tzu used the word “forget” instead of an expression like “leave” or “swim away” to emphasize the need to let go and set oneself free to pursue the Way, as “there are some things which man can do nothing about” (76). As such, one is not influenced by external forces and thus could enter the realm of complete carefree wandering (23-30). Thus, inaction is also a realization of the notion of freedom. The above symbolism of water as the Way has further led to the discourse on the implication of the Way, inaction, and brought out the central idea of freedom. Another aspect of reaching the realm of freedom is not to depend.

Chuang Tzu used water as the Way to explain the importance of non-dependence. In “Autumn Floods,” he implies that one could not discuss the ocean with a frog in a well (97), just like how a person restricted by constraints from the material world, in this case, doctrines, could not grasp the Way. To not depend, one must be inactive, and being inactive is to achieve non-dependence. Non-dependence indicates opposition to the material self (*wuwo*). It is challenging, as human beings are integrated with the world in which they are dependent on and restricted by external objects or forces and thus cannot exist independently.  As mentioned in the dialogue between Yen Yüan and Confucius in “Mastering Life,” “a good swimmer has acquired his ability through repeated practice. That means he's forgotten the water” (121). In human experience, the water could be the external forces that block people from the Way. Only by “see[ing] the water as so much dry land” (121), getting rid of the shackles and restrictions, and transcending the desires and restraints, could humans achieve an independent state of spiritual freedom. One should allow their thoughts to flow freely, unrestricted, and easily, just like water does. Chuang Tzu’s freedom is summarized as: One goes with the flow, adjusts to changes like water, and one doesn’t attach. This reflects life’s fluidity and adaptability, as water does.

Chuang Tzu’s fables are full of exaggeration, peculiarity, and absurdity. Yet, these seemingly marginal discourses have fully transmitted the key to living a life of freedom. Water symbolism is complex and multifaceted. It represents the Way, and also stimulates interesting discourses on a more detailed notion of how to obtain the Way: inaction, non-dependence and freedom. These three perspectives are interrelated and offer a comprehensive understanding of water’s significance in Chuang Tzu’s philosophy. The symbolism of water emphasizes the importance of embracing one’s natural state, allowing the mind to flow freely, and exhibiting benevolence, non-competition, and humility. Be water, my friend.
