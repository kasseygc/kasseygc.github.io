The field of contemporary Native American art is a rich and vibrant one, with a history that spans centuries and encompasses a wide range of artistic styles and mediums. Works by Indigenous artists offer valuable insights into the rich cultural heritage of Native American communities, as well as the ways in which these communities are engaging and evolving with the contemporary world through the lens of artistic representation. Contemporary Indigenous artists often use their art to challenge and resist systems of oppression, to assert their cultural identities, and to preserve their cultural traditions. Berlo summarized the post-1980s Native American art as the artists’ engagement with “their representation and misrepresentation by outsiders” (329). With that in mind, this paper aims to explore a digital video artwork, *I’ll Remember You as You Were, Not as What You’ll Become* (2016), created by artist Sky Hopinka, who is identified as a member of the Ho-Chunk Nation and the Pechanga Band of Luiseño Indians. This experimental piece offers a poetic insider’s response to the concept of ethnographical gaze and representation by incorporating elements of anthropological perspectives and verbal art into visual and aural forms.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/hopinka-2.jpg" alt="Text from Paul Radin arranged in the shape of an effigy mound"><figcaption>Still from Sky Hopinka, <em>I’ll Remember You as You Were, Not as What You’ll Become</em> (2016).</figcaption></figure>

The film, made up of nine shots, opens with a quote from an early-20th-century anthropological work by Paul Radin that describes the Ho-Chunk notion of death and immortality as a return to normal life rather than a heavenly sojourn. Hopinka uses calligrams, which are texts that take the shape of a graphic or decorative pattern, to manifest the text. It transforms the words, “…vision of a Road of Perfection which was to be rewarded by a return to earth and to the vicissitudes of the living,” into an effigy mound figure found in the Ho-Chunk region. The incorporation of anthropological archives addresses the intricacies and ambiguities of voice and representation for the artist and other Indigenous artists.

A lot of existing records about Native American culture were kept through the perspective from outsiders, mostly white, involved in a “mission” to “interpret” Indigenous lifestyles in the past (Ruíz). As for the “informants” selected by anthropologists in those studies, how representative are they of the whole tribe or the whole culture? By transforming Radin’s account of his tribe into a new artistic form that is relevant to its culture, Hopinka raises this question and acknowledges this problem of outsiders’ gaze in his work. The figure brings attention back to the Ho-Chunk Nation and its teachings as it does not concentrate on the anthropological frameworks that the words within were initially intended to support (Ruíz). We can see that the artist seeks to address the distinction between insiders’ and outsiders’ representations of Indigenous culture, and even an antithesis of ethnographical gaze and misrepresentation. This contrastive or even ironic use between words and shape gives the audience a glimpse of Hopinka’s self-representation and how he further approaches it in the rest of the film.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/hopinka-3.jpg" alt="Blurred, colour-shifted footage of pow wow dancers"><figcaption>Still from Sky Hopinka, <em>I’ll Remember You as You Were, Not as What You’ll Become</em> (2016).</figcaption></figure>

The non-traditional visual representation abstracts the whole film, pushing the creator himself and the audience away from the work. This sense of alienation and dislocation could be seen as an embodiment of the anthropological concept “ethnographic refusal” (Simpson), which refers to the case in which researchers or participants in an anthropological study refuse to engage in a certain topic or publish some specific information. Following the calligram is a long take of pow wow dance, which appears as a hyper-saturated, blurred and photonegative version with curtains of neon, bright colors. It is accompanied by the background music of a Christian Sacred Harp hymn about the search for heaven. The scene manifests a joyous, even celestial and heavenly, atmosphere. The video ends with similar footage that echoes the opening, as if it is bringing the viewers back to the start. However, the movements are slower, and the background music is more mystic in the final part. If we relate it to the Ho-Chunk concept of afterlife mentioned earlier, we can view it as a reference to the non-heavenly “normal life,” but without rose-tinted glasses.

The special filter and editing style have distanced the viewers from the screen as well as the content within. It not only creates a poetic abstraction of the pow wow that adds an additional layer to the visual representation, but also actively denies the settler or colonizer’s exoticizing gaze on this part of their culture. The artist rejects the traditional way of portraying Indigenous culture, especially in popular culture and other films, which often worsen the situation of misrepresentation or cultural appropriation in mainstream media. He does not abide by the conventions and expectations, which promise the audience access to all Indigenous knowledge, oversimplify Indigenous epistemologies, and use extractive or didactic frameworks to pacify non-Indigenous audiences (Ruíz). Instead, his work produces a unique experience for viewers, whether Indigenous or non-Indigenous, to gaze on his version of ethnographic representation from a distance, magnifying the multiplicity and complexity in it.

<figure><img src="/images/posts/hopinka-4.jpg" alt="Diane Burns reading on a dark stage beside a glowing orange cross"><figcaption>Still from Sky Hopinka, <em>I’ll Remember You as You Were, Not as What You’ll Become</em> (2016).</figcaption></figure>

The film, as described by the artist, is an elegy to the Anishinaabe and Chemehuevi poet Diane Burns. The elements of anthropological texts and abstract visual imagery greatly help intensify the content in her poetry about Indigenous cultural identity. In the third shot, she is seen in old video footage performing under the spotlight on a dark stage while reading a satirical poem made up of racist stereotypes about Indigenous people, such as “Yeah, a lot of us drink too much” and “This ain’t no stoic look. This is my face.” The verbal art performance ended in the sixth shot with another poem “Big Fun” expressing ethnic pride. During her singing-like reading, an illuminated cross is superimposing and moving across the screen, and it further moves in a circular direction, ending up turning into an illuminated circle in the color of orange, just like fire. The language in her poetry is explicit and easy to understand, without extensive figures of speech. They are genuine and simple. These performances themselves, like the poetry, shall be seen as art. Viewers could observe the emotions in her voice and her facial expression. Hopinka incorporates this footage, putting (poetry) art within (verbal) art within (video art), and thus leads us to reflect on the artistic representation of Indigenous culture metaphysically. Burns’s works serve to assert the cultural sovereignty and self-determination; while Hopinka extends them to the discussion on artists’ self-representation and the insiders’ voice.

It is challenging and never simple to bring up the topic of ethnographic representation and refusal, as well as the conundrum surrounding Indigenous history and identity. *I’ll Remember You as You Were, Not as What You’ll Become* breaks away from traditional cinematic approaches to Indigenous representation. Hopinka’s effective use of montage weaves and intertwines various visual and aural elements, which might be considered irrelevant, into a single motion picture and allows viewers from different ethnographic and cultural backgrounds to multidimensionally imagine the line of relationship within. Through time-based art, the audience could be more fully immersed in the work itself and the themes it is attempting to transmit. The motions on the screen are combined to show the core of artistic expression to history and the contemporary world, particularly with Hopinka’s poetic visual expressions, which are experimental but astonishing.
