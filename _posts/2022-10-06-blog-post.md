In her book *Minor Feelings: An Asian American Reckoning*, Korean American poet Cathy Park Hong dedicates the chapter “Bad English” to depicting the struggles, musings, tensions and consciousness among Asian American communities based on the hegemony of standard English. She provocatively proposes regarding “bad English” or nonstandard forms of English as “a weapon in a power struggle, wielding it against those who are more powerful” (Hong 101), as well as opposing white supremacy and language dominance. Meanwhile, she sheds light on a phenomenon in which second-generation Asian Americans utilize their privileges of quality education and English competence to possess power over their families. For instance, to “dispel the derision” from white interlocutors, Hong would step into the conversation and speak for her mother authoritatively (99), which could obscure and shrink the immigrant’s weakened but authentic voice.

Throughout the essay, the power dynamics evoked by distinctive English varieties are entangled with two contrasting notions—shame and pride. Readers might find it confusing after Hong proclaims that “[i]t was once a source of shame, but now [she says] it proudly: bad English is [her] heritage” (97). However, the essay does not explicitly detail or explain how the “shame to pride” transformation happens, or even her sense of pride in her language and identity, as one expects. Questions remain about the steps from shame to pride and how shame and pride are “acquired.” In response to these queries, this essay invites readers to examine the essay from a different perspective by focusing on three aspects: how Hong disguises the sources of shame in numerous tiny moments, how she vigorously utilizes shame to articulate pride and the complexity within the coexistence of shame and pride.

The context of power dynamics in homes and society should be fully grasped before interpreting the notions of shame and pride in immigrants. The text reveals Hong’s attempts to reconstruct a hierarchical power order on macro and micro levels. At the top of the hierarchy, there stands white nationalism with “good” English and a “segregated imagination” (109). In the middle are Asian Americans like Hong, who share the heritage of “bad” English but, at the same time, are privileged, educated, and capable of “good” English. At the bottom are immigrants, including the author’s family, who speak “bad” English and lack opportunities for class mobility. Individuals in the middle often face tensions between the two other parties: they are constantly navigating the outside world full of American optimism and racial injustice, as well as the intricate immigrant family dynamics filled with tensions and awkwardness. Many bring the hierarchical structure of society home. Hong admitted that she sometimes acted like a “white woman” with “strained tolerance” (99) in front of the people at the bottom of the hierarchy, being impatient and exasperated at comprehending their accents (99). In such a way, language, as a means of communication people hardly consciously attend to, becomes a tool to consolidate each position in the hierarchy and allow power to reside in invisibility.

The formation and source of shame are prominent when comprehending how shame “becomes” pride. Immigrants under “good English” sovereignty have associated shame with “bad” English. However, “bad” English is not Hong’s sole source of shame. Throughout the text, she has foregrounded the portraits of shame by articulating them in detail. Her encounters with shame could stem from an embarrassing conversation with the uncle learning a new word (93), the overuse of intimate expressions by the father (93-94), the incompetency of the mother’s English (98) or the constant seeking of approval by the grandmother (100). These sources of shame have made her “cringe” (98) and never ceased to implant themselves in her mind to a point where she would make sure “[their] inside sounds wouldn’t leak outside” (101). Readers might not notice on the first read, but Hong playfully scattered moments of shame between words in every corner, such that they are elicited throughout the essay. She recalled feeling unhappy about the prominent nose she inherited from her father (95) and projected an unrealistic body image through her anime drawings (94). Also, it irritated her that her grandmother, who had zero knowledge of English, would “confirm” the laughter with her while watching shows, “[sharpening] the cheerless tension in [their] household” (100). Through this writing strategy, Hong reminded readers that the origin of shame need not be a dramatic incident; it slips into one’s mind unnoticed through tiny moments in daily life. Hong imitated and replicated the real-life experience of unconscious shame formation and development by incorporating dispersed instances, constructing a realistic image of shame. With this realization, readers are a step further in reconstructing the path from shame to pride.

Hong then offers a more thorough insight into how shame could lead to pride. Being a speaker of both “good” and “bad” English, she shared with other Asian Americans the realization that the weight that dwells in language could be unbearable. The author grew up surrounded by “bad” English (92), well acquainted with the code-mixed, strongly accented, fragmented and ungrammatical linguistic forms, also the shame and stigma bundled with them. As she matured, she began to discern the power dynamics in society and realize that English has been dominating, colonizing and “cannibalizing” (97) other minority languages and their communities in the name of “multicultural oneness” (109). In response to that, Hong collects the nonstandard use of English and advocates the innovation of language use, as she did in her poems (100), to “queer it, twerk it, hack it, Calibanize it, other it” (97) and embrace vernacular varieties in tribute to her heritage. Without the shame brought by “bad” English, she would never have seen and utilized othering English as a creative and powerful tool to project artists’ proud voices, actively challenging concepts of cultural purity and rejecting simplistic notions of appropriation in society. Indeed, for Hong, “bad” English and the power brought by it are sources of pride. Hong’s dedication to writing poems in “bad” English, and even a book saluting her Asian American heritage, already demonstrates her pride. Initially motivated by shame, the above moments of pride have enabled language to be the outlet of the two opposing emotions. With the power gained by artistic expressions, the shame brought by “bad” English has transformed into pride. One could see that with the power obtained, shame, too, can be a source of pride.

The inquiry into the notions of Asian American shame and pride is extended and complicated when Hong draws on the cases of the half-Chinese trans artist Wu Tsang and the Latinx LGBTQ community bar Silver Platter in Los Angeles (105-108). In the weekly Wildness parties, people were connected regardless of race, gender identity and background, forming new families free from stigma (107). Nevertheless, this ideal form of a family fell apart after the transphobic review by L.A. Weekly almost ruined the bar. Wu bore the guilt that her unifying behaviors were a “gentrifying force” and had “taint[ed] all her virtuous intentions” (108). The parties were eventually halted for good. The above episode might sound detached from the overall text, but in fact, it worked as solid evidence of Hong’s perspective on the tensions and complexity of the shame she and other Asian Americans carry. In this context, a new perspective is introduced that the relationship between shame and pride is no longer just a transition or causation but also a coexistence along the journey.

The narrative portrayed similar power dynamics between families and the outside world. The shame and stigma derived from outside found their way into the household, like how they sneaked into Silver Platter. Hong further hints that second-generation Asian Americans are all “Wu Tsang” somehow. Her “shame” is a kind of “guilt” that Wu had. Like Wu’s pride in her transgender identity, she carries her pride as an Asian American. Likewise, when witnessing white individuals infantilizing and condescending to her immigrant parents, the author bore the guilt that her interference and act of overpowering using “good” English would taint her virtuous intentions of fighting back for her family. Her “virtuous intentions” signaled the pride that resided in her. Meanwhile, she was ashamed of her shame, the shame that had driven her to obscure the voices of her family. The rhetorical questions at the end of the essay, “Can I write honestly? Not only about how much I’ve been hurt but how I have hurt others?” (109), serve as her acknowledgement of the possible harm brought by her shame and that she is ashamed of it. The final components, Wu’s story and Hong’s reflective questions, energized the essay and raised its uneasiness to its fullest—although pride is backgrounded in every instance when the author mentioned her past experience, it has always coexisted with shame and never ceased to be an essential factor in her power utilization in the hierarchy.

In “Bad English,” Hong challenged us with the paradox of her struggle between shame and pride under intensive power dynamics. Her essay is not written as an Asian American pride manifesto; instead, it demonstrates the complexity of intertwined sentiments toward one’s heritage and identity. By foregrounding shame and backgrounding pride, she illustrates the development of shame and pride and problematizes their coexistence. In the world of white and English hegemony, the optimal response to such a complicated social hierarchy is to use our language, both “good” and “bad,” along with both shame and pride, as our power.

## Works Cited

Hong, Cathy Park. “Bad English.” *Minor Feelings: An Asian American Reckoning*. New York: One World, 2020, pp. 91-109.
